# Minnesota forest floor: research notes for V2 and later

Written 2026-10-04 from Matt's reference photos (`reference images/`, northern Minnesota in fall)
and the sources below. Use these notes for the V2 cutaway, the surface litter, slides and
facts. Re-check each source before a fact goes on a slide.

## The layers, top to bottom (a forest with no earthworms)

Minnesota DNR calls the worm-free hardwood forest floor **moder humus**. It "often has layers in
all stages of decomposition" (Minnesota DNR, *Humus Types*). The layers are:

| Layer | Soil name | What it looks like | Game use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **L, litter** | Oi | This fall's leaves, needles, twigs, bark and seeds, all still easy to recognise. Bright red, orange and yellow maple, aspen and birch leaves, with some still green. Older leaves bleach pale tan, and conifer needles and grass blades are mixed in. | Top of the cutaway; today's surface litter |
| **F, fermentation** | Oe | Last years' leaves: dark brown to black, wet, matted and broken into pieces. Some shapes are still recognisable. It is "permeated with fungal mycelia and root mats": white threads and fine roots run through it, with moss creeping over. | A thick dark layer under the litter, with white hyphae and rootlets |
| **H, humus** | Oa | Black, crumbly, and nothing is recognisable any more. Fine roots are dense here; "very few roots extend below the forest floor" (Worm Watch). | A thin black band above the mineral soil |
| **A, topsoil** | A | Mineral soil darkened by organic matter. It is thin in a worm-free forest; in a worm-invaded one it becomes thick, black and made mostly of casts. | The worm's main digging layer |
| **E / B, subsoil** | E, B | Lighter mineral soil (grey-white E in many northern soils, then brown to rust B), with stones and big roots. | Deeper, lighter layers; stones block digging |

- **How thick:** worm-free forest floor (L+F+H) can be "up to 10 cm (4 or 5 inches) thick in very
  rich sites dominated by sugar maple and basswood" (Worm Watch, *About the Forest*). Hale et
  al. (2005) measured forest floor up to 10 cm thick that fell to zero within 75 m at
  advancing invasion fronts. Our worm stands for 15 cm, so a full duff layer is about two
  thirds of a worm deep. That's a big world to tunnel through.
- **Why it's thick:** without earthworms, fungi and bacteria break leaves down more slowly than
  new leaves fall, so "decomposition is slower than accumulation" (Worm Watch). It acts "like a
  mulch for the forest" and holds moisture.

## It changes from place to place (Minnesota DNR humus types)

- **Under conifers or moss (mor):** a thick mat of undecomposed needles and moss, broken down
  mostly by fungi, with thin or no F and H layers and very little mixing into the mineral soil.
  In the game: a patch under fir or spruce.
- **Under hardwoods with no worms (moder):** all three layers, L, F and H. This is V2's main
  profile.
- **Under hardwoods with exotic earthworms (mull):** at most a thin layer of last fall's leaves
  over thick dark topsoil, "worm castings are usually present on the mineral soil surface"
  (Minnesota DNR). This is what invasive worms make the forest floor become. It sets up V8.

## What lives in and on it

- **Fungi:** white threads (hyphae) run through the F layer. Mushrooms, puffballs and shelf or
  crust fungi grow on litter, logs and sticks. Mycorrhizal fungi partner with tree roots, and
  earthworms disrupt those partnerships (UMN).
- **Plants:**
  - mosses (carpets, mounds and collars at tree bases, with red-stalked spore capsules);
  - clubmoss;
  - ferns;
  - wildflowers such as trillium, and seedlings that need the duff to sprout (Worm Watch).
- **Small animals (macroinvertebrates):** native land snails, spiders (wolf, jumping, orb
  weaver), beetles, ants and other insects. Diversity and numbers "can decline dramatically"
  after invasion (Worm Watch, *Macroinvertebrate Animals*).
- **Vertebrates:**
  - **Blue-spotted salamanders** "breathe through moist skin" and eat forest-floor insects, just
    like the worm needs moisture.
  - Ovenbirds nest in the thick forest floor.
  - Shrews and red-backed voles do best where the forest floor is thick.
  - Frogs and toads use the cool, moist floor (Worm Watch, *Vertebrate Animals*).
  - **Season note:** ovenbirds nest in spring and migrate in fall. Don't show an ovenbird nest in
    a fall level.
- **Logs and stones are their own habitats:**
  - Mosses and lichens cover them.
  - Puffballs, shelf and crust fungi grow on them, along with tree seedlings (nurse logs).
  - It stays damp underneath. Log-dependent insects suffer when logs dry out after the forest
    floor is lost (Worm Watch).

## What Matt's photos show (northern Minnesota, fall)

- **Mixed forest:**
  - maple, aspen and birch, with balsam fir and spruce;
  - lichen-blotched trunks with thick moss collars at the base;
  - a sapling and shrub understory, and lots of fallen sticks crisscrossing the floor.
- **Litter (2406, 2407, 2408):** a dense mosaic of red, orange, yellow, green and bleached-white
  leaves, with moss showing between them, plus grass and sedge blades and fern fronds.
- **Under the top leaves (2427, 2428, 2438):**
  - dark, wet, blackened leaves with moss creeping over them;
  - a tangle of fine roots and hyphae;
  - in 2438, black crumbly granules on the soil surface, possibly worm castings (ask Matt).
- **Fungi:**
  - pale gilled mushrooms in the litter (2414);
  - puffballs on moss and on mossy logs, some with open spore holes (2426, 2439);
  - shelf and crust fungi on fallen sticks (2419).
- **Mosses:**
  - feathery moss carpets with red-stalked spore capsules (2431, 2432);
  - a moss-covered stump with new leaves landing on it (2430);
  - a moss-covered rock or log edge with a crusty, lichen-like surface (2435, 2437);
  - clubmoss with upright cone-like spikes (2417).
- **Small plants:** seedlings with three-lobed scalloped leaves in the litter (2420) and
  five-leaflet plants (2430). Identify the species before naming them in game.
- **Animal:** a leopard frog in dead grass at an opening (2492).
